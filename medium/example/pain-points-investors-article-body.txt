After a decade of reading financial reports, many observers of China's A-share market reach the same uncomfortable conclusion: retail investors are often there to "settle the bill" — providing liquidity and capital for companies, insiders, and broader financing needs, while buy-and-hold participants frequently end up on the losing side.

That framing is harsh, but it captures a set of structural pain points that keep resurfacing in market commentary and investor conversations. Here are five of the most persistent ones.

1. A financing market, not an investment market

The primary function of A-shares, critics argue, is to help companies — especially state-owned enterprises and new listings — raise capital, rather than to deliver sustainable long-term returns to shareholders.

Frequent IPOs, refinancings, and dilutions can transfer wealth from retail investors to listed entities. Dividend payouts and shareholder protections remain weaker than in more mature markets such as the U.S. The result: even when the index rises, many individual stocks underperform, and patient investors still lose.

2. Volatility and policy-driven uncertainty

Heavy government intervention — stimulus packages, sector crackdowns, "national team" buying or selling — creates swings that feel disconnected from fundamentals. Investors describe the experience as betting on policy rather than on business quality.

Because retail participation is high, speculation, bubbles, and sharp corrections are amplified. Sophisticated investors, seeing this pattern repeat, often prefer cash, overseas assets, or alternative stores of value where they feel they have more control.

3. Poor returns and the "exit liquidity" dynamic

Years of research can lead to a sobering realization: you may be the exit liquidity for smarter players or for state financing needs. Across full cycles, many retail investors lose money despite occasional bull runs. Wealth transfers from small accounts to institutions, insiders, and participants who benefit from issuance events.

4. Closing overseas channels (2026 repatriation pressure)

Recent crackdowns on platforms such as Futu, Tiger Brokers, and Longbridge — and pressure around roughly $1 trillion in repatriation — have forced many investors to sell overseas holdings (U.S. and Hong Kong stocks) over a multi-year window, often on a "sell only, no buy" basis.

That means missing strong performers in markets like Nasdaq tech while being pushed back into A-shares they already distrust. Legal alternatives such as QDII funds and Stock Connect exist, but quotas, costs, and limited choice remain constraints.

5. Broader economic trust and capital-control anxiety

A property slump and uneven economic growth make domestic assets less appealing to many wealthy households. Capital controls, currency volatility, and fear of further restrictions push some toward cash or crypto to preserve optionality for future outflows. Low financial literacy among parts of the retail base amplifies behavioral biases and losses.

Why cash and crypto look attractive to some wealthy investors

Investors who have compared A-shares with overseas markets often cite better governance, dividends, and growth narratives elsewhere. They view domestic equities as too speculative and prefer liquidity plus diversification over forced domestic exposure.

Overall sentiment: repatriation alone is unlikely to sustainably "save" A-shares without deeper reforms. Short-term liquidity may help, but without structural change, the "settling the bill" dynamic for ordinary investors may simply continue.

These pain points help explain capital outflows in 2025 and ongoing skepticism toward the market's role as a long-term wealth-building vehicle for retail participants.
